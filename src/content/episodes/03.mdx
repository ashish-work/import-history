## The World

In March 2005, Herb Sutter published "The Free Lunch Is Over" in Dr. Dobb's Journal. The argument: clock speeds have stalled; from now on, the future is cores. For fifteen years, slow software had simply waited for faster chips. That subsidy just ended.

The hardware followed on schedule. Intel's Core Duo shipped in January 2006, and by 2008 every laptop was multicore. Python's founding assumption from 1991 — one core per machine, so one big lock costs nothing — broke overnight. Nobody changed a line of Python; the machines changed underneath it.

Then, in November 2009 at JSConf EU, Node.js arrived, selling single-threaded async as a brand. Suddenly Python's web story had outside competition wearing its own old clothes.

## The Pressure

The GIL went from footnote to villain. On a multicore machine, CPU-bound Python simply cannot scale up — the second core sits dark while the lock passes hand to hand. Meanwhile the web tier needed C10K-class concurrency; Twisted existed, but its callback style split the community into those who would contort their code and those who refused.

## The Response

The community didn't route around the GIL once. It did it three ways simultaneously.

**Sidestep it — processes.** `multiprocessing` landed in 2.6 and 3.0 in 2008 (PEP 371, adopted from the pyprocessing package). Be clear about what it is: not a fix, a workaround — with serialization taxes on every object crossing the boundary. It's also why a decade of tutorials cargo-cult `if __name__ == "__main__":` without explaining it.

**Hide it — implicit green threads.** greenlet, extracted from Stackless ideas by Armin Rigo, begat eventlet — born at Linden Lab to run Second Life — which begat gevent, Denis Bilenko's 2009 monkey-patch-the-world answer: blocking code that doesn't block. Tornado, open-sourced by FriendFeed in 2009, staked out the pragmatic middle path.

**Fix the GIL itself — and fail, mostly.** Antoine Pitrou's new GIL (3.2, 2011) moved to time-slice scheduling and fixed multicore thrashing — then Beazley's 2010 follow-up exposed the convoy effect with I/O threads. Google's Unladen Swallow (2009) promised a 5x LLVM JIT with a merge plan in PEP 3146, and fizzled by 2011, leaving a published retrospective. Psyco was retired in favor of PyPy, which got genuinely fast around 2010–11 and became the perennial "just use PyPy" answer that production mostly doesn't take.

## The Fight

The fight had a professor. Beazley's "Inside the Python GIL" (ChiPy, 2009) and "Understanding the Python GIL" (PyCon 2010) did what nobody had bothered to do in nineteen years: instrument the lock and show the room what it actually did.
