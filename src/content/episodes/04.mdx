## The World

MATLAB licenses cost thousands of dollars per seat, and academics were priced out. Fortran and C were fast and hostile. Between them sat the "two-language problem": prototype in something humane, then rewrite everything in something fast — twice the work, twice the bugs.

The demand was real and institutional: astronomy at the Hubble's science institute, physics labs everywhere — all needing array computing on a budget.

## The Pressure

What scientists needed was specific: interactive exploration, fast arrays, plotting, and a language you could teach a grad student in a week. Here's the strange part — nobody built Python for this. The pressure found the language.

## The Response

The scientific stack assembled itself one defection at a time.

**Arrays.** Jim Hugunin's Numeric (1995) split when the Hubble institute built numarray (2001) — until Travis Oliphant unified the two into NumPy in 2005–06. Mark this: a community schism, healed. Remember it during episode 5.

**Plotting.** matplotlib (2003), built by John Hunter — a MATLAB refugee doing epilepsy research. He died in 2012; NumFOCUS named its award for him.

**Interactivity.** IPython began in 2001 as Fernando Pérez's physics-PhD procrastination project. It became the Notebook in 2011, then Jupyter in 2014–15 — the lab notebook that became the industry's default interface.

**Tables.** pandas, open-sourced 2009–10; McKinney's "Python for Data Analysis" (2012) converted an industry.

**Machine learning.** scikit-learn — a Google Summer of Code project in 2007, released by INRIA in 2010 — whose fit/predict API design later frameworks would imitate.

**Packaging the pain away.** Compiled extensions were hell to install, so Anaconda and conda arrived in 2012 (Continuum — Oliphant and Peter Wang), wheels standardized binaries the same year (PEP 427), and Cython (2007, out of Pyrex from 2002) became the on-ramp for C-glue.

## Why Your Code Looks Like This

This is the thesis chapter. Here is the mechanism, stated plainly: **C extensions release the GIL.** When NumPy dives below the lock into compiled code, other threads run. The meter climbs.

Follow the chain. The GIL forced heavy compute below the line. A world-class C and Fortran ecosystem accreted down there. Science chose Python *because of that ecosystem*. The flaw caused the win.

That's why `import numpy as np` is the real Python logo. That's why your "Python" hot loop is actually BLAS. That's why `@` exists as an operator — matrix multiplication, PEP 465: numpy literally got syntax added to the language. The ecosystem now steers the core. Hold that thought until episode 8.
